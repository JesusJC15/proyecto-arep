# Cardiac Symptoms and Chest Pain

Chest pain is a cardinal symptom of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and other life-threatening conditions. However, musculoskeletal and functional causes are more frequent in primary care. Accurate risk stratification guides disposition and urgency of evaluation.

## Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)

ACS encompasses unstable angina (UA), ST-elevation MI (STEMI), and non-ST-elevation MI (NSTEMI). Typical presentation is chest discomfort or pressure, often radiating to the left arm, jaw, or back. Associated symptoms include dyspnea, diaphoresis (sweating), nausea, and fatigue. Discomfort is often described as pressure, heaviness, or squeezing rather than sharp pain.

Atypical presentations are common, especially in women, diabetics, and elderly patients. In these groups, dyspnea, fatigue, nausea, or syncope may be the dominant symptom without prominent chest discomfort.

**IMMEDIATE ESCALATION** triggers:
- Chest pressure or discomfort with any of: dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, syncope
- Symptoms worsened by exertion or emotional stress
- Symptoms relieved inconsistently or not at all by rest
- History of hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidemia, or prior MI
- Age >50 (men) or >60 (women)
- Symptom duration >15–20 minutes

## Palpitations and Arrhythmias

Palpitations are awareness of heartbeat. Benign causes include sinus tachycardia, anxiety, and caffeine sensitivity. Pathologic causes include atrial fibrillation (AF), supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), and ventricular ectopy.

Red flags indicating arrhythmia vs. anxiety:
- Syncope or presyncope with palpitations
- Marked dyspnea accompanying irregular heartbeat
- Chest pain or pressure during episodes
- Palpitations triggered by exertion (suggests SVT or VT)
- Family history of sudden cardiac death or inherited arrhythmia
- Duration >5–10 minutes at rest

Atrial fibrillation (AF) presents with irregular, rapid palpitations and may cause dyspnea, chest discomfort, or syncope. AF increases stroke risk significantly; new-onset AF requires evaluation and anticoagulation assessment.

## Myocarditis and Pericarditis

Myocarditis (inflammation of cardiac muscle) often follows viral illness. Presentation includes chest pain (often pleuritic or positional), dyspnea, palpitations, and syncope. Fulminant myocarditis can cause cardiogenic shock and sudden death. Escalate if chest pain with dyspnea, syncope, or signs of heart failure.

Pericarditis (inflammation of pericardium) causes sharp, pleuritic chest pain relieved by sitting up and leaning forward. Pericardial friction rub may be heard on auscultation. Pericardial effusion can progress to tamponade (hypotension, elevated JVP, muffled heart sounds), a life-threatening emergency.

## Non-Cardiac Chest Pain

Musculoskeletal pain is reproducible on palpation and worsened by movement or deep breathing. It is not associated with dyspnea, diaphoresis, or nausea. Reassurance and NSAIDs are typically sufficient.

Acid reflux (GERD) causes burning epigastric or retrosternal discomfort, often worse after meals or when recumbent. Relief with antacids or PPI therapy supports the diagnosis. GERD is not associated with exertional onset or dyspnea at rest.

Anxiety and panic disorder can mimic ACS, causing chest tightness, dyspnea, palpitations, and diaphoresis. However, anxiety typically develops acutely in response to stress, resolves within minutes to hours, and lacks exertional trigger. Any doubt warrants EKG and troponin evaluation.

## Risk Stratification Framework

**High-risk features** (escalate urgently):
- Age >50 (men) or >60 (women)
- Hypertension, diabetes, smoking, or hyperlipidemia
- Prior MI, stroke, or coronary revascularization
- Dyspnea, diaphoresis, or nausea with chest discomfort
- Symptoms triggered by exertion
- Symptoms at rest lasting >20 minutes
- Syncope or presyncope

**Lower-risk features** (may observe with close follow-up):
- Young age (<40 without risk factors)
- Pleuritic or positional pain (relieved by position change)
- Reproducible tenderness on palpation
- Symptoms triggered solely by anxiety or stress
- Associated symptoms (dyspnea, nausea, diaphoresis) are absent

## Self-Care Guidance

Musculoskeletal chest pain responds to rest, NSAIDs, and topical heat. Most cases resolve within 1–2 weeks. GERD symptoms improve with dietary modification, smaller meals, avoiding trigger foods, and elevating the head of the bed.

For anxiety-related symptoms, grounding techniques, slow breathing (4-4-4 breathing), and reassurance are helpful. However, any doubt about cardiac etiology mandates professional evaluation.

## Emergency Warning Signs

**Call emergency services immediately** if:
- Chest pressure or discomfort with dyspnea, sweating, or arm pain
- Syncope or presyncope
- Severe dyspnea at rest
- Palpitations with syncope or severe dyspnea
- Chest pain with hypotension or altered mental status

## Escalation Pathway

Refer for professional evaluation if:
- Chest discomfort is new, recurring, or progressive
- Associated dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea, or syncope
- History of cardiac risk factors or prior cardiac events
- Exertional chest discomfort
- Palpitations lasting >5–10 minutes or recurring frequently
- Any high-risk feature identified during assessment

## Follow-Up

Patients with chest pain attributed to musculoskeletal or anxiety causes should follow up if pain persists >2 weeks, worsens, or acquires new features (exertional onset, dyspnea, diaphoresis). Any escalation in severity warrants urgent evaluation.
