# Fever Management and Infection Screening

Fever is a common presenting symptom across all age groups and etiologies. While most febrile illnesses are self-limited viral infections, fever can herald serious bacterial or invasive infections requiring urgent intervention.

## Fever Definition and Physiology

Fever is defined as core body temperature ≥38°C (100.4°F). Measured temperature varies by site: oral, tympanic, and axillary measurements are most common in ambulatory settings. Rectal temperature is the gold standard but less practical.

Fever is typically a host defense mechanism that enhances immune responses. However, very high fever (>40°C) can cause delirium, seizures (in young children), and rhabdomyolysis.

## Viral vs. Bacterial Infections

**Viral infections** are the most common cause of fever, accounting for 80–90% of undifferentiated fevers in primary care. Typical features: gradual onset, constitutional symptoms (malaise, myalgias, headache), often with URI or GI symptoms. Cough, sore throat, or diarrhea may predominate. Lymphocytosis on CBC is common.

**Bacterial infections** often present with more abrupt onset, focal symptoms (dysuria, cough with sputum, localized pain), and elevated WBC with left shift. Severity often correlates with bacterial etiology.

## Fever in Young Children

Young children (<3 years) with fever warrant careful evaluation because serious bacterial infections (meningitis, bacteremia, urinary tract infection, pneumonia) are more common and harder to detect clinically.

**Fever criteria for urgency** (PECARN guidelines, age 3–36 months):
- Fever without source and appearance: fully well, nontoxic appearance suggests low risk for serious bacterial infection
- Fever with focal findings (ear, throat, lung) guides diagnosis
- Fever with toxic appearance (lethargy, poor perfusion, difficulty rousing) mandates urgent evaluation
- Fever >39°C is not independently a red flag if child appears well, but full evaluation is warranted

## Fever in Adults

Adult patients with fever and clear source (pharyngitis, otitis media, pneumonia, UTI) are managed based on severity of that condition. Fever of unknown origin (FUO) is fever >38.3°C for ≥3 weeks without diagnosis after initial evaluation.

## Common Infectious Etiologies

**Upper Respiratory Infections**: Fever with cough, sore throat, nasal congestion. Most viral; rarely bacterial (streptococcal pharyngitis). Self-limited over 3–7 days.

**Influenza**: Fever, malaise, myalgias, nonproductive cough. Onset is abrupt. Antiviral therapy (oseltamivir) is most effective if started within 48 hours.

**Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP)**: Fever, cough (productive or dry), dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain. Focal infiltrate on exam or imaging supports diagnosis. CURB-65 score guides admission decision.

**Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs)**: Dysuria, frequency, urgency. Fever suggests pyelonephritis rather than simple cystitis. Costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness suggests upper UTI. Pyuria on urinalysis supports diagnosis.

**Meningitis**: High fever, severe headache, neck stiffness (nuchal rigidity), altered mental status, petechial or purpuric rash. Meningitis is a medical emergency; lumbar puncture and empiric antibiotics are indicated.

**Gastroenteritis**: Fever, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea. Most cases are viral and self-limited. Bloody diarrhea or fever >3 days suggests bacterial etiology and warrants stool studies.

**Skin and Soft Tissue Infections**: Fever with localized erythema, warmth, swelling, or drainage suggests cellulitis or abscess. Erythema migrans with or without fever suggests Lyme disease (tick-borne).

## Fever in Immunocompromised Patients

Patients with HIV (CD4 <200), neutropenia, or on immunosuppressive therapy can develop unusual infections. Fever in these patients warrants urgent evaluation and broad-spectrum coverage until diagnosis is established.

## Malaria and Tropical Infections

In patients with travel history to endemic regions, fever may represent malaria, dengue, yellow fever, or other exotic infections. Detailed travel history and appropriate serologic or molecular testing are essential.

## Red Flags Requiring Immediate Evaluation

- Fever with altered mental status or confusion
- Fever with petechial or purpuric rash (meningococcemia, rickettsial disease)
- Fever with severe headache and neck stiffness (meningitis)
- Fever with severe dyspnea (pneumonia, myocarditis)
- Fever with severe abdominal pain and peritoneal signs (peritonitis, appendicitis)
- Fever with hypotension or signs of sepsis
- High fever (>40°C) with altered mental status (heat stroke, malignant hyperthermia—rare in infection)
- Fever in pregnancy with abdominal pain or vaginal bleeding

## Self-Care for Mild-to-Moderate Fever

Acetaminophen or ibuprofen can reduce fever and associated discomfort. Fluids, rest, and light clothing support comfort. Most viral fevers resolve within 3–5 days. Prophylactic antimicrobials are not indicated for viral infections.

## Escalation Pathway

**Escalate urgently** if:
- Any high-risk feature above is present
- Fever >40°C with altered mental status
- Fever with respiratory distress or hypoxia
- Fever with hypotension or signs of shock
- Fever in young children (<3 years) without clear source
- Fever in immunocompromised patients
- Fever >39°C that does not respond to antipyretics
- Fever lasting >5–7 days without diagnosis

**Watchful waiting** is appropriate for:
- Fever ≤38.5°C with clear source (URI, mild gastroenteritis)
- Well-appearing child with fever and clear viral URI symptoms
- Adult with fever and clear localized infection (pharyngitis, otitis) without systemic toxicity
- Fever responding to antipyretics with patient able to tolerate fluids and maintain hydration

**Return precautions**:
- Return if fever persists >5–7 days
- Return if new focal symptoms develop (difficulty breathing, severe headache, rash)
- Return if patient becomes difficult to arouse or confused
- Return if unable to tolerate fluids and at risk for dehydration
- Return if breathing becomes labored or chest pain develops

## Follow-Up Recommendations

Patients sent home with fever should follow up in 24–48 hours to ensure improvement and no new concerning features. Particularly important in young children and elderly patients.

## Fever in Special Populations

**Pregnant women**: Fever warrants prompt evaluation to exclude chorioamnionitis, pyelonephritis, or other obstetric emergencies. Acetaminophen is safe; NSAIDs are generally avoided, especially in third trimester.

**Elderly patients**: Fever may be blunted in elderly, and serious infections may present with lower-grade fever or nonspecific symptoms (weakness, confusion). Any fever in an elderly patient warrants evaluation.

**Post-operative patients**: Fever within 24–48 hours post-op is often from surgical site infection or anesthesia. Fever later may indicate deeper infection, sepsis, or VTE. Close monitoring is warranted.
