# Gastrointestinal Symptoms and Abdominal Pain

Abdominal pain is a common presenting symptom with a broad differential diagnosis. Accurate characterization of location, quality, duration, and associated symptoms guides severity assessment and disposition.

## Acute Gastroenteritis

Viral gastroenteritis (norovirus, rotavirus, adenovirus) and bacterial gastroenteritis (Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella) present with nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal cramping. Fever is common. Most cases are self-limited, lasting 24–72 hours. Fluid loss can lead to dehydration, particularly in young children, elderly, or those with comorbidities.

Severity assessment:
- **Mild**: Diarrhea ≤3 stools/day, mild cramping, no fever or low-grade fever, no signs of dehydration
- **Moderate**: Diarrhea 4–6 stools/day, moderate cramping, fever, mild dehydration (mild orthostasis, decreased skin turgor)
- **Severe**: Diarrhea >6 stools/day, severe cramping, high fever, signs of moderate-to-severe dehydration (hypotension, altered mental status), bloody diarrhea, severe vomiting preventing oral intake

## Peptic Ulcer Disease and GERD

Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) causes epigastric pain, often burning in quality. Pain is typically worse between meals (duodenal ulcer) or with meals (gastric ulcer). Associated symptoms include nausea, early satiety, and weight loss if chronic. Acute bleeding presents with hematemesis (vomiting blood) or melena (black, tarry stool).

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) causes retrosternal or epigastric burning, worse after large meals, spicy foods, or when recumbent. Relief with antacids or PPI therapy supports the diagnosis. Complications include erosive esophagitis, Barrett's esophagus, and stricture formation.

## Appendicitis

Classic presentation is periumbilical pain that migrates to the right lower quadrant (RLQ), accompanied by nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and low-grade fever. McBurney's point tenderness (one-third distance from ASIS to umbilicus) is characteristic. Rebound tenderness and guarding suggest peritoneal irritation.

Atypical presentations occur if the appendix is retrocecal, pelvic, or if the patient is pregnant. High clinical suspicion warrants imaging (CT abdomen/pelvis) to confirm or exclude appendicitis.

**RED FLAGS**: 
- Acute RLQ pain with rebound/guarding
- Fever with localized abdominal tenderness
- Vomiting preventing oral intake with abdominal pain

## Cholecystitis and Biliary Colic

Acute cholecystitis results from cystic duct obstruction by gallstone, causing acute inflammation. Typical presentation is right upper quadrant (RUQ) pain, often radiating to the right shoulder or back, triggered by fatty meals. Associated symptoms include nausea, vomiting, and low-grade fever. Pain usually persists >30 minutes (distinguishing from biliary colic).

Biliary colic (uncomplicated gallstone disease) causes episodic RUQ or epigastric pain lasting 30 minutes to several hours, often at night, without fever or elevated inflammatory markers.

**RED FLAGS**: 
- RUQ pain with fever and elevated WBC (suggests acute cholecystitis)
- RUQ pain with jaundice (suggests choledocholithiasis with obstruction)
- Severe pain with hypotension and altered mental status (suggests perforation or sepsis)

## Pancreatitis

Acute pancreatitis presents with epigastric pain radiating to the left flank or back, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and abdominal distension. Serum lipase is markedly elevated (>3 times upper limit of normal). Severity is assessed by APACHE II or modified Marshall score.

Common etiologies: gallstones (50%), alcohol (35%), hypertriglyceridemia, medications, idiopathic. Severe pancreatitis can progress to organ failure, shock, and death.

**RED FLAGS**: 
- Severe epigastric pain radiating to back
- Vomiting with inability to tolerate oral intake
- Abdominal distension and decreased bowel sounds
- Hypotension or altered mental status

## Bowel Obstruction

Mechanical bowel obstruction presents with abdominal pain (colicky or constant), abdominal distension, nausea, vomiting, and absence of bowel movements (obstipation). Vomiting is typically later in ileal obstruction and earlier in proximal obstruction.

**RED FLAGS**: 
- Severe, persistent abdominal pain with distension
- Inability to pass stool or flatus
- Bilious vomiting
- Visible peristaltic waves or palpable mass

## Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

Ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease cause chronic inflammation, resulting in recurrent episodes of diarrhea (often bloody in UC), abdominal pain, tenesmus (painful urge to defecate), and weight loss. Fever, perirectal fistulas, or erythema nodosum may be present.

Acute flare presents with increased frequency of bowel movements, urgency, blood in stool, and crampy abdominal pain.

## Functional Dyspepsia and IBS

Functional dyspepsia causes upper abdominal discomfort or burning without organic pathology. Epigastric pain, early satiety, bloating, and nausea are common. Symptoms are chronic and recurrent.

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) causes abdominal pain or discomfort associated with altered bowel habits (diarrhea, constipation, or alternating). Urgency, mucus in stool, and bloating are typical. Symptoms are chronic, relapsing-remitting, and worse with stress.

Both conditions have negative diagnostic workup (normal labs, imaging) and are diagnoses of exclusion after serious pathology is ruled out.

## Risk Stratification Framework

**HIGH-RISK abdominal pain** (escalate urgently):
- Severe pain with rebound tenderness or guarding
- RUQ pain with fever (acute cholecystitis)
- Epigastric pain radiating to back with elevated lipase (pancreatitis)
- Abdominal distension with inability to pass stool (obstruction)
- Acute abdominal pain with hypotension or altered mental status
- Bloody diarrhea with fever and systemic signs

**MODERATE-RISK pain** (professional review recommended):
- Acute onset epigastric pain with nausea/vomiting
- RLQ pain with fever (appendicitis concern)
- Abdominal pain with persistent high fever (>39°C)
- Diarrhea >6 stools/day with dehydration signs

**LOW-RISK pain** (watchful waiting with precautions):
- Mild cramping with 1–3 stools of diarrhea, no fever
- Epigastric burning relieved by antacids (GERD)
- Postprandial fullness or bloating without alarm features
- Reproducible trigger (e.g., fatty food followed by cramping)

## Hydration and Self-Care

Rehydration with electrolyte solutions (oral rehydration therapy, ORT) is first-line for gastroenteritis. Small, frequent meals of bland foods (crackers, rice, applesauce, toast) are tolerated once vomiting subsides. Avoid dairy, fat, and high-fiber foods until recovery.

Antacids or H2-blockers may relieve GERD symptoms. NSAIDs can worsen PUD and should be avoided if ulcer disease is suspected.

## Escalation and Return Precautions

**Escalate immediately** if:
- Severe abdominal pain with rebound/guarding
- Fever >39°C with localized pain
- Bloody diarrhea or hematemesis
- Persistent vomiting preventing oral intake (risk of severe dehydration)
- Signs of dehydration (orthostasis, altered mental status, decreased urine output)
- Abdominal distension with absence of bowel sounds

**Watchful waiting** is appropriate for mild-to-moderate gastroenteritis, GERD, or functional pain **if**:
- Patient can tolerate fluids orally
- No fever or low-grade fever only
- No signs of dehydration
- No alarm features (blood, severe pain, peritoneal signs)
- Patient can return if worsening

Return immediately if symptoms worsen, fever develops or persists >3 days, unable to tolerate fluids, or signs of dehydration appear.
