# Musculoskeletal Pain and Injuries

Musculoskeletal pain is the most common reason for ambulatory visits. Accurate assessment of injury type, severity, and red flags guides management and disposition.

## Muscle Strain and Ligament Sprain

Muscle strain (muscle tear) occurs from overstretching or overexertion. Ligament sprain (ligament tear) occurs from excessive force through a joint. Both present with pain, swelling, bruising, and reduced range of motion.

**Severity graded 1–3**:
- Grade 1: Mild pain, no loss of strength or stability, minimal swelling
- Grade 2: Moderate pain, some loss of strength or stability, moderate swelling
- Grade 3: Severe pain, complete loss of function, marked swelling

**Management**:
- Rest, ice (first 48 hours), compression, elevation (RICE)
- NSAIDs for pain and inflammation
- Gradual return to activity as pain allows
- Physical therapy for severe sprains or recurrent injuries

Most strains and uncomplicated sprains resolve within 2–6 weeks with conservative management.

## Fractures

Fractures present with severe pain, swelling, deformity, inability to bear weight or use limb, and often bruising. Crepitus (bone grinding) or abnormal mobility may be palpated.

**Risk factors**: Trauma (high-energy in young, low-energy in elderly with osteoporosis), osteoporosis, pathologic fracture (cancer, osteomyelitis).

**Red flags**:
- Inability to bear weight or move limb
- Severe swelling or deformity
- Neurovascular compromise (absent pulse, altered sensation, cold limb)
- Open fracture (bone exposed through skin—medical emergency)
- Fracture of pelvis or femur (high risk for hemorrhage)

**Management**: Immobilization, elevation, analgesia, imaging to confirm diagnosis and guide surgical vs. conservative management.

Stress fractures (repetitive microtrauma) present with gradual onset localized pain, worse with weight-bearing or activity, improving with rest. Common in athletes and military personnel. Diagnosis confirmed by imaging (may require MRI if X-ray negative).

## Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) Tear

ACL tear presents with sudden pain, audible "pop" at time of injury, rapid swelling, and inability to bear weight or pivot. Lachman test and anterior drawer test are positive on exam.

**Management**: Acute RICE, crutches, surgical referral for evaluation. Conservative management (bracing, physical therapy) may be appropriate for less active patients; surgery is typical for athletes.

## Meniscal Tear

Meniscal tear presents with pain, swelling, and locking or catching sensation in the knee. McMurray test may be positive. Often associated with ACL injuries (unhappy triad: ACL, meniscus, and MCL tear).

## Patellar Dislocation

Patellar dislocation presents with sudden lateral displacement of patella, severe pain, swelling, and inability to extend knee. Often reduces spontaneously or with gentle knee extension.

**Management**: RICE, analgesia, immobilization, orthopedic referral. Recurrent dislocations may require surgery.

## Rotator Cuff Tears

Rotator cuff tears present with shoulder pain (often worse at night), weakness with abduction or external rotation, and difficulty reaching overhead or behind back.

**Acute tears**: May present after traumatic injury with sudden severe pain.

**Chronic tears**: Insidious onset with progressive weakness and pain. More common with age and repetitive overhead activity.

**Management**: Physical therapy for partial tears; surgery often required for full-thickness tears causing significant functional impairment.

## Osteoarthritis and Rheumatoid Arthritis

Osteoarthritis (OA) is degenerative joint disease causing morning stiffness (usually <30 minutes), pain with activity, worse at end of day, and reduced range of motion. Commonly affects weight-bearing joints (knees, hips, lower back) and hands.

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) causes symmetric joint pain and swelling (often hands, wrists, feet), morning stiffness (>1 hour), systemic symptoms (fever, fatigue, weight loss), and elevated inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, positive rheumatoid factor or anti-CCP).

## Gout

Gout presents with sudden-onset severe pain and swelling of a joint, often the first MTP joint (podagra). Associated warmth, erythema, and inability to bear weight. Often triggered by purine-rich foods, alcohol, dehydration, or diuretic use.

**Diagnosis**: Synovial fluid analysis shows needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals.

**Management**: NSAIDs or colchicine for acute attack, prophylactic therapy to lower uric acid if recurrent.

## Low Back Pain (LBP)

Low back pain is mechanical (85% of cases) or radicular (<15%). Mechanical LBP is associated with poor posture, weak core muscles, or minor strain. Pain is localized to lower back, worse with certain movements, improving with rest.

Radicular pain radiates down the leg (sciatica) due to nerve root compression, often from herniated disc or spinal stenosis.

**Red flags for serious pathology** (cancer, infection, fracture, cauda equina syndrome):
- Pain onset after age 50 or <20 years
- Fever with back pain (infection)
- History of cancer with new back pain
- IV drug use with back pain (concern for osteomyelitis)
- Immunosuppression (HIV, transplant) with back pain
- Severe neurologic deficits (progressive weakness, bilateral leg pain, bowel/bladder dysfunction)
- Midline tenderness or neurologic examination abnormality
- Progressive pain despite conservative management

**Cauda equina syndrome** (surgical emergency):
- Acute severe back pain with bilateral leg pain and numbness
- Bowel/bladder dysfunction (retention or incontinence)
- Saddle anesthesia (numbness in perianal area)
- Requires urgent MRI and likely surgical decompression

**Management**: Most mechanical LBP improves with activity (avoid prolonged bed rest), NSAIDs, physical therapy, and core strengthening.

## Compartment Syndrome

Compartment syndrome is increased pressure within a closed anatomic compartment (leg, forearm, hand), compromising blood flow and tissue viability. Presents with pain out of proportion to findings, pain with passive stretch of muscles in the compartment, and progressive neurologic or vascular compromise.

**RED FLAG**: Compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency requiring urgent fasciotomy to prevent permanent tissue damage.

## Risk Stratification

**HIGH-RISK musculoskeletal injury** (escalate urgently):
- Fracture suspected with inability to bear weight or severe swelling
- Open fracture or neurovascular compromise
- Severe swelling with pain out of proportion (concern for compartment syndrome)
- Patellar dislocation
- Acute rotator cuff tear with significant weakness
- Cauda equina syndrome symptoms
- Any red flag for serious pathology with back pain

**MODERATE-RISK injury** (professional review):
- Significant swelling and pain limiting function
- Suspected ligament tear (ACL, meniscus)
- Inability to bear weight or use limb after 24–48 hours of RICE
- Mechanical pain unresponsive to conservative measures >2 weeks

**LOW-RISK injury** (self-care with precautions):
- Mild strain or sprain with minimal swelling and preserved function
- Pain responding to RICE and NSAIDs
- Gradual return to activity without worsening

## Self-Care

RICE (rest, ice, compression, elevation) for first 48 hours reduces pain and swelling. Gradual return to activity as pain allows, avoiding re-injury. Physical therapy and core strengthening prevent recurrence.

## Escalation and Follow-Up

Return to professional evaluation if:
- No improvement after 2–3 weeks of conservative management
- Worsening swelling, redness, or warmth (concern for infection)
- Neurologic symptoms develop (numbness, tingling, weakness)
- Inability to bear weight persists >2–3 days
- New limitation of function develops

Most uncomplicated strains and sprains resolve within 2–6 weeks; residual stiffness may persist longer.
