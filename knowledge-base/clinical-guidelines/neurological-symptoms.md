# Neurological Symptoms: Headache, Dizziness, Syncope

Neurological symptoms are common presenting complaints that range from benign to life-threatening. Accurate characterization and red flag identification are critical.

## Headache Classification

**Primary headaches** (no structural underlying disease):
- **Tension-type headache**: Bilateral, pressing or tightening quality, mild to moderate intensity, lasting 30 minutes to 7 days. Associated with stress, poor posture. Nausea is usually absent.
- **Migraine**: Unilateral, throbbing, moderate to severe intensity, lasting 4–72 hours. Associated nausea, photophobia, phonophobia. Often preceded by aura (visual flashing, fortification spectra). Migraine affects 12–15% of population; more common in women.
- **Cluster headache**: Unilateral orbital pain, severe, lasting 15–180 minutes. Associated ipsilateral lacrimation, nasal congestion, eye redness. Clustered over weeks to months followed by remission periods.

**Secondary headaches** (from underlying condition):
- Meningitis: Fever, headache, neck stiffness, altered mental status. Meningitis is a medical emergency.
- Intracranial hemorrhage (SAH): Sudden, severe "thunderclap" headache, often described as "worst headache of life." Associated neck stiffness, photophobia, altered mental status, focal neurologic deficits.
- Hypertensive emergency: Severe headache with systolic BP >180 and diastolic >120, often with vision changes or altered mental status.
- Temporal arteritis: Headache in patient >50 years with new temporal pain, jaw claudication, visual symptoms (amaurosis fugax), or systemic symptoms (fever, malaise, myalgias).
- Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH): Chronic headache worse in morning or with Valsalva, vision changes, tinnitus. Young, obese women are typical demographic.

## Red Flags for Headache

**Urgent escalation required**:
- Sudden-onset severe headache (thunderclap) with or without focal neurologic deficits, altered mental status, or seizure
- Headache with fever and neck stiffness (meningitis)
- Headache with focal neurologic deficits (stroke, hemorrhage)
- Headache with altered mental status or confusion
- Headache with severe hypertension (>180/120)
- New headache pattern or worst headache of life
- Headache in immunocompromised patient or with history of malignancy (consider infection, metastasis, leptomeningeal disease)
- Headache after head trauma
- Progressive headache worsening over weeks to months with neurologic decline

**Watchful waiting** appropriate for:
- Typical migraine with aura (known pattern)
- Tension-type headache responsive to rest and analgesics
- Mild headache without fever, focal neurologic deficits, or red flags

## Dizziness and Vertigo

Dizziness is a non-specific complaint; characterization is essential.

**Vertigo** (room spinning):
- **Peripheral vertigo**: BPPV (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo) causes brief vertigo with head position change, often preceded by head trauma or prolonged bed rest. Nystagmus can be reproduced with Dix-Hallpike maneuver.
- **Vestibular neuronitis**: Abrupt onset vertigo, often following viral infection, with nausea and vomiting. Usually resolves over days to weeks.
- **Meniere's disease**: Episodic vertigo lasting hours, associated with unilateral hearing loss, tinnitus, aural fullness.
- **Central vertigo**: Stroke, MS, or brainstem lesion. Associated focal neurologic deficits (dysarthria, dysdiadochokinesia, nystagmus with fast phase away from lesion).

**Presyncope** (feeling faint without loss of consciousness):
- Orthostatic hypotension: Dizziness upon standing from lying or sitting. Associated pallor, diaphoresis, tachycardia.
- Cardiac arrhythmia: Palpitations with presyncope.
- Hyperventilation: Associated paresthesias, hand cramping (carpopedal spasm), triggered by anxiety.
- Hypoglycemia: Tremor, diaphoresis, tachycardia, confusion in severe cases.

**Non-vertiginous dizziness** (lightheadedness, imbalance):
- Anemia, dehydration, medications, or psychiatric conditions

## Syncope (Fainting)

Syncope is transient loss of consciousness with spontaneous recovery. Prodrome (presyncope) often precedes loss of consciousness.

**Reflex syncope** (most common, 50%):
- **Vasovagal syncope**: Triggered by emotional stress, sight of blood, or pain. Prodrome of dizziness, palpitations, nausea. Sudden sympathetic withdrawal and parasympathetic surge cause hypotension and bradycardia.
- **Situational syncope**: Syncope triggered by coughing, urination, defecation, or swallowing.

**Orthostatic hypotension** (10%):
- Symptomatic drop in systolic BP ≥20 mmHg or diastolic ≥10 mmHg upon standing. Associated with dehydration, medications (antihypertensives, diuretics, antidepressants), autonomic neuropathy.

**Cardiac syncope** (15–20%, most serious):
- Arrhythmia (SVT, VT, bradycardia, prolonged QT)
- Structural disease (aortic stenosis, HOCM, PE, tamponade)
- Characterized by abrupt loss of consciousness without prodrome, rapid recovery. May have brief convulsive movements (convulsive syncope) mimicking seizure.

**Neurogenic syncope** (seizure, TIA, migraine):
- Seizure often has prodrome (aura), followed by convulsive movements, tongue biting, incontinence. Post-ictal confusion lasting minutes to hours distinguishes from syncope.

## Red Flags for Dizziness and Syncope

**Urgent evaluation required**:
- Syncope with abrupt onset, no prodrome (cardiac concern)
- Syncope with palpitations or chest discomfort
- Syncope with dyspnea or hemoptysis
- Syncope with focal neurologic deficits (stroke)
- Recurrent syncope episodes
- Syncope with sudden death risk factors (family history, prior syncope, structural heart disease)
- Vertigo with focal neurologic deficits, ataxia, or altered mental status (central cause)
- New neurologic deficits (weakness, numbness, speech difficulty) with dizziness

## Self-Care for Benign Dizziness

**For BPPV**: Head repositioning (Epley maneuver if trained) can resolve symptoms. Vestibular rehabilitation exercises promote central compensation.

**For vestibular neuronitis**: Time and vestibular rehabilitation are primary treatments. Antiemetics and benzodiazepines (meclizine) can provide symptom relief.

**For orthostatic hypotension**: Increased fluid intake, salt, compression stockings, and slow position changes help. Recumbency during prodrome can prevent syncope.

**For migraine-associated dizziness**: Migraine prophylaxis and acute treatments reduce frequency and severity.

## Escalation Pathway

**Escalate immediately** if:
- Sudden severe headache with focal neurologic deficits
- Headache with fever and stiff neck
- Syncope with palpitations, chest discomfort, or cardiac risk factors
- Any loss of consciousness with delayed recovery (>minutes)
- Vertigo with focal neurologic findings
- Recurrent syncope or presyncope

**Watchful waiting** appropriate for:
- Typical migraines or tension headaches responsive to rest and analgesics
- BPPV with reproducible triggers and short duration
- Presyncope from known orthostatic hypotension in compliant patient

## Follow-Up Recommendations

Patients with first syncope episode warrant outpatient follow-up (24–72 hours) to discuss triggers, risk stratification, and need for EKG or imaging. Recurrent syncope or syncope with cardiac risk factors warrants urgent cardiology referral.

Chronic headaches warrant follow-up to assess response to preventive therapy and rule out secondary causes if pattern changes.
