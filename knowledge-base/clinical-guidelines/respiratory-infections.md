# Respiratory Tract Infections

Respiratory tract infections (RTIs) range from self-limited upper respiratory illness to life-threatening lower respiratory disease. Accurate risk stratification is critical because early deterioration can progress rapidly, yet most RTIs require supportive care without antibiotics.

## Upper Respiratory Infections

Common cold, pharyngitis, and rhinosinusitis are viral in >80% of cases. Symptoms include nasal congestion, sore throat, cough, and low-grade fever. Most resolve within 7–10 days without intervention. Red flags that escalate from watchful waiting: difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), drooling, trismus (jaw stiffness), or rapid progression to difficulty breathing.

Streptococcal pharyngitis presents with severe sore throat, fever, and lymphadenopathy but generally lacks URI symptoms. Confirmed group A streptococcus (by rapid antigen test or throat culture) warrants antibiotic therapy to prevent complications like rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. Absence of cough, coryza, and hoarseness favor bacterial infection.

## Lower Respiratory Infections

Acute bronchitis typically presents after upper respiratory symptoms with persistent cough lasting 2–8 weeks. Most cases are viral; bacterial superinfection is uncommon in immunocompetent hosts. Cough is the cardinal feature; dyspnea, chest pain, and wheezing are present if airway inflammation is marked. Recovery is expected without antibiotics unless patient is older or has underlying lung disease.

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) combines fever, cough (productive or dry), dyspnea, and focal or diffuse infiltrates on imaging. Severity is stratified by CURB-65 score (confusion, urea >7, respiratory rate ≥30, blood pressure <90/60 or diastolic <60, age ≥65). Score 0–1: outpatient management; 2: consider admission; 3–5: hospital admission. Hypoxia (SpO2 <90%), respiratory distress (RR >30), or hemodynamic instability mandate immediate escalation.

## Warning Signs for Escalation

Escalate immediately if the patient reports:
- Severe dyspnea at rest or with minimal exertion
- Stridor or tripoding (sitting upright unable to recline)
- Chest pain pleuritic or positional
- Hemoptysis (blood in sputum)
- Altered mental status or confusion
- High fever (>39°C) with hypotension or signs of sepsis
- Cyanosis or pallor
- SpO2 <90% on room air

Watchful waiting with clear return precautions is appropriate for mild-to-moderate URI or early bronchitis without dyspnea, hypoxia, or risk factors.

## Hydration and Self-Care

Fluids, rest, and acetaminophen or ibuprofen for symptom relief are first-line. Honey or cough lozenges may ease discomfort. Avoid smoking and air pollution. Humidified air can reduce airway irritation. Symptoms should improve within 3–5 days; persistent or worsening cough warrants reevaluation.

## Escalation and Follow-Up

Refer for professional review if:
- Shortness of breath is progressive
- Fever persists >3–5 days or recurs after defervescence
- Cough changes character (e.g., becomes blood-tinged)
- New chest pain or pleurisy appears
- Patient is elderly, pregnant, or immunocompromised
- Symptoms interfere with sleep or daily activities for >2 weeks

Early follow-up (2–3 days) is prudent for elderly patients or those with comorbidities like COPD or heart failure.
