# Skin and Soft Tissue Infections

Skin and soft tissue infections range from superficial folliculitis to life-threatening necrotizing fasciitis. Depth and extent of involvement guide classification and urgency.

## Impetigo

Impetigo is a superficial infection of the epidermis, most common in young children. Non-bullous impetigo (70%) presents with honey-crusted lesions on exposed areas (face, extremities). Bullous impetigo presents with fragile bullae that rupture, leaving erosions.

**Causative organisms**: Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA), Group A Streptococcus.

**Management**: Topical antibiotics (mupirocin) for localized disease; systemic antibiotics for widespread impetigo. Avoid spreading through good hygiene and handwashing.

## Folliculitis

Folliculitis is infection of hair follicles, presenting as small pustules or papules at follicle sites. Usually caused by Staphylococcus aureus. Localized disease responds to topical antibiotics or drainage if purulent.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa folliculitis (hot tub folliculitis) occurs after exposure to warm water with inadequate chlorination.

## Cellulitis

Cellulitis is acute inflammation of dermal and subcutaneous tissue. Presents with localized erythema, warmth, edema, and tenderness. Borders are typically ill-defined (contrasting with erysipelas, which has well-demarcated raised borders).

**Common causative organisms**: 
- Group A Streptococcus (most common)
- Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA)
- Other gram-negative rods or anaerobes (if puncture wound or water-related)

**Risk factors**: Diabetes, obesity, immunosuppression, venous insufficiency, prior cellulitis, lymphedema, skin barrier disruption (wound, insect bite, athlete's foot).

**Severity**: Uncomplicated cellulitis in immunocompetent patient without systemic toxicity may be treated outpatient with oral antibiotics. Complicated cellulitis (large area, rapid spread, fever, hypotension, immunosuppression) warrants hospitalization and IV antibiotics.

## Abscess

Abscess is a collection of pus within tissue, presenting as a localized swelling, warmth, fluctuance, and pain. Drainage of pus may occur spontaneously or via minor procedures. Antibiotics alone are insufficient; drainage is required.

Small abscesses (<5 cm) may respond to incision and drainage alone; larger abscesses may require imaging to localize and guide drainage.

## Erysipelas

Erysipelas is a superficial streptococcal infection presenting with raised, well-demarcated erythema often on the face or lower extremities. Associated fever and lymphadenopathy are common. More common in young children and elderly.

## Lymphangitis

Lymphangitis is infection of lymphatic vessels, presenting as red streaking extending from the site of primary infection (wound, cellulitis) toward regional lymph nodes. Associated fever, chills, and regional lymphadenopathy are present.

Lymphangitis indicates more serious infection and systemic involvement; IV antibiotics are warranted.

## Diabetic Foot Ulcer and Infection

Diabetic patients are at high risk for foot ulcers and infections due to sensory neuropathy (impaired pain sensation) and vascular disease. Infection can progress rapidly, leading to deep tissue involvement, osteomyelitis, and amputation if untreated.

**Wagner classification**:
- Grade 0: No ulcer
- Grade 1: Superficial ulcer
- Grade 2: Ulcer extending to tendon or ligament
- Grade 3: Ulcer with osteomyelitis or abscess
- Grade 4: Gangrene of part of foot
- Grade 5: Gangrene of entire foot

Any ulcer with cellulitis, warmth, or purulent drainage requires urgent evaluation and IV antibiotics. Vascular and orthopedic involvement assessment may be necessary.

## Necrotizing Fasciitis

Necrotizing fasciitis is a rapidly progressive deep tissue infection with high mortality (20–30%). Presents with severe pain out of proportion to physical findings, swelling that progresses rapidly, skin changes (erythema, bullae, crepitus), and systemic toxicity (fever, tachycardia, hypotension).

**Causative organisms**: 
- Type 1: Polymicrobial (gram-positive, gram-negative, anaerobes)
- Type 2: Group A Streptococcus ± Staphylococcus aureus
- Type 3: Gram-negative (Vibrio, Aeromonas) after water exposure
- Type 4: Fungal (rare)

**Risk factors**: Diabetes, immunosuppression, recent surgery, penetrating trauma, injection drug use.

**RED FLAG**: Necrotizing fasciitis is a medical emergency requiring urgent surgical debridement and IV antibiotics. High index of suspicion is warranted if pain is severe relative to physical findings.

## Wound Infection

Post-operative wound infections occur at incision site within days to weeks of surgery. Presents with erythema, warmth, edema, purulent drainage, and fever. Superficial infection may be managed with drainage and topical antibiotics. Deep infection requires IV antibiotics and possible return to operating room.

## Risk Stratification

**HIGH-RISK skin/soft tissue infection** (escalate urgently):
- Rapid spread of erythema or edema
- Severe pain out of proportion to findings (suspect necrotizing fasciitis)
- Systemic toxicity: fever >39°C, hypotension, tachycardia, altered mental status
- Lymphangitis (red streaking toward lymph nodes)
- Immunocompromised patient with cellulitis or abscess
- Diabetes with any foot ulcer with signs of infection
- Water-related infection (concern for gram-negative organisms)
- Signs of deep tissue involvement (fluctuance, crepitus)

**MODERATE-RISK infection** (professional review):
- Localized cellulitis with fever but stable vital signs
- Cellulitis in immunocompromised or diabetic patient
- Large or spreading area of erythema
- Abscess that has not drained spontaneously

**LOW-RISK infection** (may observe with care):
- Small folliculitis or impetigo in immunocompetent person
- Localized cellulitis <5 cm without fever, stable vital signs
- Known abscess drained with appropriate antibiotic coverage

## Self-Care

Impetigo and superficial infection: Good hygiene, avoid picking or scratching, topical antibiotics, and isolation to prevent spread.

Cellulitis: Elevation of affected area, rest, and oral antibiotics if appropriate for severity.

## Prevention

Maintain skin integrity, promptly clean wounds, and use appropriate wound care. In diabetic patients, careful foot inspection daily and prompt treatment of minor wounds reduce infection risk.

## Follow-Up

Most cellulitis improves within 48–72 hours of appropriate antibiotic therapy. If no improvement or worsening, reassess for complications (deep infection, abscess, necrotizing fasciitis) and consider imaging or blood cultures.

Recurring cellulitis (same location repeatedly) warrants investigation for chronic lymphedema, venous insufficiency, or other predisposing factors.
