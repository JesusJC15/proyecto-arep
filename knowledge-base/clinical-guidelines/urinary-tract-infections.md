# Urinary Tract Infections and Dysuria

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) range from asymptomatic bacteriuria to urosepsis. Clinical presentation and risk factors guide diagnosis and management.

## Cystitis (Lower UTI)

Acute cystitis presents with dysuria (pain on urination), frequency (urinating >8 times daily), urgency (acute need to urinate), and suprapubic pain or pressure. Fever is typically absent in uncomplicated cystitis but may be present.

**Risk factors**: Female sex, sexual activity, spermicide use, urinary retention, neurogenic bladder, pregnancy.

**Diagnosis**: Urinalysis with pyuria (WBC >5/hpf) and bacteriuria supports diagnosis. Urine culture is gold standard but not necessary for uncomplicated cystitis in non-pregnant women with classic symptoms.

**Severity**: Uncomplicated cystitis is not associated with systemic toxicity, fever, or flank pain. Presence of these features suggests upper UTI (pyelonephritis) or complicated infection.

## Pyelonephritis (Upper UTI)

Acute pyelonephritis presents with fever, flank pain (often unilateral), costovertebral angle (CVA) tenderness on exam, nausea, vomiting, and dysuria. Systemic symptoms (malaise, rigors) may be present.

**Risk factors**: Diabetes, pregnancy, obstruction, recurrent UTIs, neurogenic bladder, immunosuppression.

**Severity**: Uncomplicated pyelonephritis in non-pregnant women without risk factors may be managed outpatient with oral antibiotics. Complicated pyelonephritis (fever >39°C, unable to tolerate oral intake, immunocompromised, obstruction, signs of sepsis) requires hospitalization and IV antibiotics.

**RED FLAGS**:
- Fever >39°C with flank pain and CVA tenderness
- Nausea/vomiting preventing oral intake
- Hypotension or tachycardia
- Altered mental status
- Signs of urosepsis

## Asymptomatic Bacteriuria

Bacteriuria without symptoms is common, especially in elderly and diabetic patients. Treatment is **not** recommended except in pregnant women and patients undergoing urologic procedures.

## Prostatitis

Acute bacterial prostatitis presents with dysuria, frequency, urgency, perineal pain, and fever. Digital rectal exam (DRE) reveals a swollen, tender prostate. Urine or blood cultures may grow pathogenic bacteria.

Chronic prostatitis causes recurrent dysuria, perineal pain, and lower back pain. Bacterial causes are less common; chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS) is more frequent.

**RED FLAGS**: 
- High fever with perineal pain and sepsis signs (acute bacterial prostatitis with urosepsis)
- Urinary retention with sepsis

## Risk Stratification

**Uncomplicated UTI** (outpatient management):
- Dysuria, frequency, urgency without fever or flank pain
- Non-pregnant, non-diabetic, no obstruction or neurogenic bladder
- Able to tolerate oral medications

**Complicated UTI** (consider hospitalization):
- Fever >38.5°C with flank pain (pyelonephritis)
- Pregnant with pyelonephritis
- Diabetes or immunosuppression with fever
- Unable to tolerate oral intake
- Signs of systemic toxicity or sepsis
- Obstruction suspected (anuria, severe flank pain)

## Self-Care and Prevention

Hydration (increased water intake), frequent urination, and voiding after intercourse reduce UTI risk. Cranberry juice or supplements may reduce recurrent UTI incidence in some studies, though evidence is mixed.

Urinary analgesia (phenazopyridine, 200 mg three times daily) reduces dysuria discomfort but colors urine orange.

**Prevention strategies**:
- Adequate hydration
- Regular urination, especially after intercourse
- Wipe front to back after defecation
- Avoid prolonged urinary retention
- Consider prophylactic antibiotics for frequently recurrent UTIs

## Escalation Pathway

**Escalate urgently** if:
- Fever >39°C with flank pain and systemic toxicity signs
- Unable to tolerate oral intake
- Hypotension, tachycardia, or altered mental status
- Urinary retention with fever
- Immunocompromised patient with fever and dysuria
- Pregnant with signs of pyelonephritis
- Male with fever and dysuria (concern for prostatitis)

**Professional review recommended** if:
- First episode of pyelonephritis
- Recurrent UTIs (≥2 in 6 months, ≥3 in 12 months)
- Dysuria with inability to characterize source (UTI vs. other cause)
- Dysuria in male
- Dysuria during pregnancy
- Failure to improve after empiric antibiotic therapy

**Watchful waiting** appropriate for:
- Classic uncomplicated cystitis in non-pregnant woman
- Mild dysuria with clear UTI symptoms, no fever
- Able to tolerate fluids and oral medications

## Follow-Up and Monitoring

Follow-up urinalysis is not necessary after completion of antibiotic course if symptoms resolve.

Recurrent UTI workup (≥3 in 12 months in women, ≥1 in 12 months in men) includes imaging (renal ultrasound or CT) to exclude obstruction, stones, or anatomic abnormality.

Pregnant women with treated UTI should have repeat urinalysis 1–2 weeks after therapy to confirm cure.
